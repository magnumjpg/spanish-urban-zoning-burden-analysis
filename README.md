An advanced econometric and public choice research project quantifying the causal price distortions, supply inelasticity, and deadweight loss generated by municipal discretionary land-use planning (Plan General de Ordenación Urbana - PGOU) across 52 Spanish municipalities over a 15-year longitudinal panel (2009-2023, N = 780).

---

## Executive Overview

Across OECD economies, housing affordability is often attributed to external demand shocks or speculative financial cycles. In Spain, however, empirical evidence demonstrates that the crisis is fundamentally institutional and regulatory: under the national land framework (Texto Refundido de la Ley de Suelo, RDL 7/2015) and autonomous urban statutes, all land is legally classified as non-buildable (suelo no urbanizable) by default.

To develop land, parcels must undergo discretionary political reclassification (recalificación) by municipal councils through local General Urban Plans (PGOU). This statutory monopoly grants local governments discretionary authority to restrict building volumes, producing artificial land scarcity, administrative backlogs of 12 to 24 months for building permits, and speculative land value leaps exceeding 24x.

This study models and contrasts two institutional arrangements:
1. The Discretionary Municipal Monopoly (Status Quo PGOU): Political rationing of development rights, multi-year permitting queues, and inelastic supply.
2. The By-Right Liberalized Baseline (Counterfactual): A property-rights baseline where all non-ecologically protected land is developable by default, licenses are issued within 2.5 months through technical verification, and developers respond elastically to demand.

Key empirical findings:
- The Permitting Delay Tax: Under a Two-Way Fixed Effects (TWFE) panel specification with clustered standard errors, each additional month of municipal licensing backlog raises housing prices by +1.53% ceteris paribus (p < 0.001). In major metropolitan areas with 18 to 22 months of delay, administrative friction explains 22% to 26% of acquisition costs.
- Artificial Land Freezing: A 10 percentage point increase in the proportion of administratively frozen rustic land produces a +2.69% price increase (p = 0.0056).
- Supply Elasticity Suppression: In Tier 1 Metros (Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia, Málaga, Palma), price elasticity of supply under PGOU is suppressed at epsilon_s = 0.31 (matching Banco de España estimates). Under By-Right, elasticity expands 3.8x to epsilon_s = 1.20, dampening price surges through production.
- Cumulative Welfare Destruction (2009-2023): Discretionary zoning generated a cumulative burden of 73.59 billion euros across the 52 municipalities (29.13 billion euros in Harberger Deadweight Loss and 44.46 billion euros in Tullock Rent Dissipation), representing 4,381.95 euros per resident.

## 1. The Empirical Puzzle: Spain's Territorial Paradox

Conventional demographic metrics often obscure real settlement patterns due to the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP). When examining population through the standardized 1 km² grid developed by Eurostat and the Joint Research Centre (GEOSTAT Population Grid), Spain exhibits one of the most extreme settlement paradoxes in the developed world:

- 87.0% of Spanish land is completely uninhabited: Out of 505,990 km² of national territory, only 13.0% of 1 km² cells contain registered residents. Spain is the most spatially concentrated nation in the European Union.
- Europe's highest effective residential density: While gross density appears low at 93 hab/km², effective residential density in inhabited cells reaches 737 hab/km², surpassing the Netherlands (647 hab/km²), Germany (390 hab/km²), and France (175 hab/km²).
- High-rise vertical living: 65.7% of Spaniards reside in multi-family apartment blocks (versus a 47.5% EU average), driving metropolitan densities in Barcelona and Madrid to between 25,000 and 45,000 hab/km² in residential zones.
- Cumulative housing deficit of 750,000 homes: According to the Banco de España Annual Report 2025 presentation by David López Salido (Director General of Economics, June 18, 2026, Slide 29), the cumulative gap between net household creation and completed dwellings over 2021-2025 reached approximately 750,000 units (-3.9% of average households).
- Crushing effort ratios: The Banco de España (Slide 43) records that tenant households in Madrid, Barcelona, and Málaga now require 10.0 years of total net income to purchase an average home, compared to 4.0 years for existing owner-occupiers nationwide.

## 3. Econometric Identification & Price Determinants

To identify the causal effect of planning delays and artificial scarcity on housing prices, we specify a Two-Way Fixed Effects (TWFE) panel model across 52 representative municipalities over 15 years (2009-2023, N = 780):

$$\ln(P_{it}) = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta_1 \text{Delay}_{it} + \beta_2 \text{Frozen}_{it} + \beta_3 \text{Premium}_{it} + \beta_4 \ln(\text{Corruption}_{it}) + \beta_5 \ln(\text{Income}_{it}) + \beta_6 \text{PopGrowth}_{it} + u_{it}$$

Where:
- alpha_i captures municipality fixed effects (controlling for time-invariant geography, coastal proximity, and amenities).
- lambda_t captures year fixed effects (non-parametrically absorbing ECB interest rate cycles, national inflation, and construction input cost shocks).
- Standard errors are clustered at the municipal level (52 clusters) to provide robust inference against arbitrary serial correlation.

## 4. Supply Inelasticity & The Municipal Bottleneck

When demographic inflows accelerate, housing affordability depends on the price elasticity of supply:

$$\epsilon_s = \frac{\% \Delta \text{Housing Starts}}{\% \Delta \text{Real Price}}$$

Under the discretionary PGOU framework, municipal zoning caps residential expansion regardless of price signals. In Tier 1 Metros, estimated supply elasticity is constrained at epsilon_s = 0.31, preventing homebuilding from matching household growth. Under a counterfactual By-Right regime, supply elasticity expands to epsilon_s = 1.20 (and up to 1.44 in dynamic tourist hubs), enabling production to buffer demand shocks.

A Wald hypothesis test confirms the structural break in supply elasticity:

$$Z = \frac{\epsilon_s^{\text{ByRight}} - \epsilon_s^{\text{PGOU}}}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(\epsilon_s^{\text{ByRight}}) + \text{Var}(\epsilon_s^{\text{PGOU}})}} = 2.137 \quad (p = 0.0326)$$

## 5. Welfare Economics: Deadweight Loss & Tullock Rent Dissipation

Applying the welfare framework of Paul Cheshire and Stephen Sheppard (2002) and Gordon Tullock's (1967) theory of rent dissipation, we decompose the total social cost of municipal discretionary land-use planning:

### 1. Harberger Deadweight Loss (Unrealized Trades)
The triangle of net consumer and producer surplus destroyed because artificial scarcity quotas prevent mutually beneficial housing transactions from occurring:

$$DWL_{it} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot (P_{it}^{\text{PGOU}} - P_{it}^{\text{ByRight}}) \cdot (Q_{it}^{\text{ByRight}} - Q_{it}^{\text{PGOU}}) \cdot S$$

### 2. Tullock Rent Dissipation (Wealth Extraction)
The rectangular transfer extracted from home buyers and tenants, dissipated through unproductive municipal lobbying, legal appeals, financial carrying costs of locked land, and developer land-banking:

$$\text{Rent Transfer}_{it} = (P_{it}^{\text{PGOU}} - P_{it}^{\text{ByRight}}) \cdot Q_{it}^{\text{PGOU}} \cdot S$$

Where S = 95 m² is the average family dwelling surface area (INE Housing Census).

### Cumulative Burden Breakdown (2009-2023)
- Harberger Deadweight Loss: 29.13 billion euros
- Tullock Rent Dissipation: 44.46 billion euros
- Total Cumulative Burden: 73.59 billion euros (4,381.95 euros per resident across the panel)

## 6. Policy Counterfactuals: 10,000-Run Monte Carlo Simulation

To test policy interventions without assuming uniform municipal behavior, we executed a stochastic Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 draws. The simulation models three concrete institutional reforms incorporating parameter uncertainty, orographic absorption constraints, and non-linear supply responses:

1. Scenario 1 (Fast-Track Permitting): Enforcing a binding 3-month positive administrative silence (declaración responsable) with professional liability certification. Compresses financial carrying costs.
2. Scenario 2 (Peri-Urban Land Unfreezing): Automatic reclassification of 60% of common rustic land adjoining municipal transportation corridors, with log-normal orographic filtering.
3. Scenario 3 (Comprehensive By-Right Reform): Full property rights transition combining 2.5-month permitting, buildability by default, and structural supply elasticity expansion (epsilon_s >= 1.20).

## 7. Conclusions & Strategic Policy Roadmap

1. Unaffordability is not driven by physical land scarcity: 87% of Spain is unpopulated. The housing crisis is an institutional failure created by the discretionary planning monopoly established in RDL 7/2015.
2. Inefficacy of demand subsidies: In markets with rigid supply elasticity (epsilon_s = 0.31 to 0.45), rent vouchers and public purchase guarantees are capitalized directly into higher prices without creating physical homes.
3. Supply unlocking at zero fiscal cost: Adopting a By-Right regime with positive administrative silence can lower metropolitan prices by up to 38.5% and close 90% of the annual housing backlog without requiring public debt or budget allocations.
